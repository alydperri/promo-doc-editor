# Rubric

The evaluation criteria for promotion documents. Each rule defines what good looks like and what to flag. The editor applies these during critique — every finding must cite a rule from this file.

---

## Section-specific rules

### Scope of Role

**R1: About the job, not the person.**
This section describes the role, not the employee's performance in it. It reads like a job description. If the employee's name appears, or if performance language creeps in ("she excels at..."), it fails this rule. It should be possible to hand this section to any person filling the role.

**R2: Specific, not generic.**
This section must describe the scope of THIS specific role, not a generic description of the role type. Restating the leveling guide verbatim is a fail. The reader needs to understand what this role looks like in this specific team, org, or business context.

**R3: Criteria-aligned.**
The scope description should connect to the expectations for the proposed level. The scope should demonstrate that the role's responsibilities, complexity, and influence match the next-level expectations.

### Promotion Assessment

**R4: Balanced.**
This is not a sales pitch. The assessment should include all relevant details and results, even negative ones. If every example is a pure win with no nuance, flag it. If an example omits what went wrong or what the employee struggled with, flag it.

**R5: Objective.**
The assessment must describe performance, not the person. Watch for:
- Exaggerated language ("flawless," "practically perfect," "amazing")
- Claims without supporting evidence ("she sets the bar for the team")
- Opinion presented as fact without examples

Every claim needs a specific, data-backed example. "Nira sets the bar for coding" is an opinion. "Nira's code reviews on Project X caught sub-par code, and her insistence on quality reduced post-launch bugs by 20%" is evidence.

**R6: Aligned to company values.**
Examples should connect the employee's performance to the organization's shared values or principles. Flag examples that describe good work but don't tie it to the shared vocabulary. See `../rules.md` for how to handle this rule when no company values template is provided.

**R7: Criteria-focused.**
Examples must evaluate performance against next-level expectations, not current-level excellence. "She's the best L5 we have" is not evidence of L6 readiness. The assessment must reference what the next level requires and show the employee meeting those requirements. Tenure and time in role are never valid criteria.

**R8: Specific.**
Every example must answer: What was the situation? What did the employee do? What was the result? If any of those three are missing, the example is incomplete. Flag vague impact statements ("drove significant results") and demand data, scope, or measurable outcomes. The SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact) or STAR (Situation-Task-Action-Result) framework is the standard.

**R9: Stands on its own.**
A reviewer outside the writer's org should be able to read this and understand the impact. Flag:
- Undefined acronyms (spell them out on first use)
- Team-specific jargon without explanation
- References to internal tools, projects, or processes that aren't described
- Assumptions that the reader knows the business context

**R10: Clear and concise.**
Good writing is easy to read. Flag:
- Overly complex sentences that need deciphering
- Filler language that doesn't advance the argument
- Paragraphs without a clear topic sentence
- Walls of text with no structure or formatting
- Redundant examples that make the same point

### Growth Areas

**R11: No missing growth areas.**
Every employee has areas for growth at the next level. If a draft claims there are none, reject it outright. This section exists to show the writer thought critically about the promotion decision. Omitting growth areas is not acceptable.

**R12: Objective (growth areas).**
Same standard as R5 — focus on performance, not personality. "He's just not a detail person" is about the person. "He has missed reporting deadlines on three occasions in the last quarter" is about performance.

**R13: Criteria-focused (growth areas).**
Growth areas must be relevant to the expectations of the proposed level and role. Tenure is never a valid growth area. "She's only been in the role 6 months" is not criteria-focused. Flag growth areas that don't connect to what success looks like at the next level.

**R14: Specific (growth areas).**
Vague growth areas are useless. "He needs to improve his communication" — how? In what context? With what stakeholders? What specific behaviors need to change? Demand the same SBI/STAR level of detail for growth areas as for strengths.

**R15: Addresses how gaps are being closed.**
It's not enough to name a gap. The writer must describe what the employee and manager are doing about it. "We spoke about it and he's aware" is not a plan. "He will attend two workshops this quarter and we review his progress monthly" is a plan.

**R16: Incorporates feedback.**
If promotion feedback has been received, this section should reference it. Look for:
- Trends across multiple feedback providers being acknowledged
- Direct quotes from feedback used to support or contextualize the growth area
- Conflicting feedback being addressed (not ignored or dismissed)
- Feedback that raises growth areas not covered by the writer being acknowledged somewhere

Bad: "I don't agree with Sally's feedback. She doesn't know the whole story."
Better: "As Sally noted, John has room to improve here. He's aware of this and we've been working on it through coaching."

### Promotion Feedback (standalone)

**R17: Balanced feedback.**
All promotion feedback must include both Reasons to Promote AND Reasons Not to Promote. If either section is missing, flag it. If a feedback provider hasn't identified any growth areas, they may not be the right person to provide feedback — flag this.

**R18: Clear vote.**
Feedback should begin with a clear position: support or do not support the promotion. Fence-sitting without a clear recommendation weakens the feedback.

## Universal writing rules

These apply to every section:

**W1: Clarity above all.**
Good writing is transparent. If a sentence requires re-reading to understand, it fails. Every structural choice, word choice, and formatting decision should serve clarity.

**W2: Active voice.**
Default to active voice. "The project was delivered by Sarah" should be "Sarah delivered the project." Passive voice obscures who did what.

**W3: No jargon without context.**
If a term isn't universally understood, define it on first use or replace it with a common equivalent.

**W4: Cut what doesn't serve.**
Every sentence must earn its place. If removing a sentence doesn't weaken the argument, remove it.

**W5: No format over substance.**
Bold text, bullet points, and headers are organizational tools, not substitutes for strong writing. A well-formatted list of vague claims is still vague.
