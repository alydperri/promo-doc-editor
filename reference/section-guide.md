# Section Guide

A reference for what each section of the promotion document should accomplish. Use this to evaluate whether a section meets its intended purpose.

---

## 1. Scope of Role

**Purpose:** Establish that the employee's role is scoped at the next level.

**What it contains:**
- A description of the role's responsibilities, complexity, influence, and impact
- If applicable: leadership responsibilities (team size, geographic distribution, management structure)

**What it should read like:** A job description. It describes what the role requires, independent of who fills it.

**Common failures:**
- Writing about the person instead of the role (names, performance language)
- Copying the leveling guide verbatim instead of describing THIS specific role
- Using vague, generic language that could describe any role ("manages complexity," "navigates change")
- Failing to provide enough context for an outside reviewer to understand the role

**Recommended length:** Under 500 words.

---

## 2. Promotion Assessment

**Purpose:** Provide evidence that the employee is already performing at the next level.

**What it contains:**
- Specific examples of next-level performance
- Examples connected to company values (if available) and leveling expectations
- Data and measurable outcomes demonstrating impact

**What it should read like:** A well-structured argument with evidence. Each paragraph should have a clear claim supported by a specific example.

**Common failures:**
- Listing accomplishments without connecting them to next-level criteria
- Subjective praise without supporting evidence
- Using current-level excellence as proof of next-level readiness ("best L5 we have")
- Omitting negative details from otherwise positive examples (breaking balance)
- Making claims that assume the reader knows the business context

**Structure tip:** A strong format states the next-level expectation, then follows with a specific example demonstrating how the employee meets it. If a leveling guide is available, use its criteria as the organizing structure.

---

## 3. Growth Areas

**Purpose:** Show that the writer has thought critically about this promotion and honestly assessed where the employee still needs to develop at the next level.

**What it contains:**
- A description of the areas where the employee needs to grow at the next level
- A description of how those growth areas are being addressed (specific plans, not vague awareness)
- Acknowledgment of relevant promotion feedback

**What it should read like:** An honest, forward-looking development assessment. It's not a sales pitch; it's a sign of mature judgment.

**Common failures:**
- Claiming there are no growth areas (rejection-level)
- Using emotional appeals ("they deserve it," "they've worked so hard")
- Listing growth areas but not describing remediation plans
- Using tenure as a growth area (not criteria-focused)
- Dismissing or ignoring feedback that raises concerns
- Being vague ("needs to improve communication")

---

## 4. Feedback

**What it contains (when soliciting):**
- Feedback from qualified colleagues at or above the proposed level
- Both Reasons to Promote (RTP) and Reasons Not to Promote (RNTP) from each provider
- A diverse set of perspectives reflecting a holistic view of the employee's performance

**What it contains (when providing):**
- A clear vote (support or do not support)
- Specific examples supporting the vote
- Both RTP and RNTP, regardless of the vote
- Writing that follows all the same quality standards as the rest of the doc

**Common failures for feedback providers:**
- Omitting RNTP entirely ("I can't think of any growth areas")
- Being vague ("great team player")
- Using personality descriptions instead of performance examples
- Providing feedback at the current level instead of evaluating against the next level

**Common failures for managers incorporating feedback:**
- Ignoring feedback that conflicts with their assessment
- Dismissing critical feedback defensively
- Failing to reference feedback trends across multiple providers
- Not using feedback to strengthen the rest of the document

---

## General writing principles

Across all sections, George Orwell's rules remain the standard:

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Use active voice whenever possible.
5. Never use jargon if you can think of an everyday equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.
